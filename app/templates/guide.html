How Handover works

When someone leaves a project, what they knew leaves with them. Handover turns their notes, chat and tasks into a short list of facts, each one tied to the exact words it came from, so the next person can trust it.

The story in this demo

{{ project.name }} is an invented campus app for finding club events and booking seats. Riya Sharma built the backend and leaves on 20 November. Kabir Mehta takes over. Meera Iyer leads the club and approves what Riya leaves behind. Mr. Rao is a mentor who may only see a limited view. Everything here is made up.

What happens, in order

  1. Sources go in. Meeting notes, team chat, a task list and a code-activity export. See them under Sources (admin).
  2. Claims come out. A claim is one plain statement, such as a decision, an owner, a risk or an open question. These are the rows in the Review queue.
  3. Every claim shows its proof. The system looks for the claim's quote in the original source. If the words are really there, it is marked quote-verified. If not, it says so.
  4. A person decides. A reviewer approves, edits or rejects each claim. Nothing is trusted just because software wrote it.
  5. A release is published. The approved claims become a numbered handover. It cannot be changed afterwards.
  6. The new owner acknowledges it. Kabir reads his view and confirms, and the confirmation is recorded with his name.

What the coloured labels mean

  • quote-verified The exact quote was found in the source. This checks the words, not the meaning, which is why a person still approves it.
  • inferred The source suggests it but does not say it directly. Check it before approving.
  • conflicting Two sources disagree. A reviewer must pick which is right. See Conflicts.
  • unsupported No source backs it up. Treat it as a guess until someone finds proof.

The pills for team, restricted and confidential are sensitivity levels. Each role can only read up to its own level, and anything higher is hidden, not blurred.

What this does not do

It does not decide what is true. It shows where each statement came from, flags weak ones, and makes a human responsible for the final call.
